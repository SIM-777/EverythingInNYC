How Much Does a Trip to NYC Really Cost?

📅 Updated 2026 · ⏱️ 11 minute read

People ask me this more than almost anything else: “how much money do I actually need for New York?” I get it — this city has a reputation for swallowing an entire vacation budget in a weekend if you let it. It can. It also doesn’t have to. I’ve done this city on $110 a day with a hostel bunk and a dollar slice for dinner, and I’ve done it on $500+ a day with a room overlooking the park and an orchestra seat on Broadway. Both trips were great. They were just completely different trips.

So instead of giving you one vague number, I’m giving you four — broken down by exactly where the money goes, based on what things actually cost right now. No padding, no “it depends,” just real ranges you can plan around.

What You’re Actually Paying For

Where You Sleep

Here’s the thing nobody tells you: the biggest lever in your entire NYC budget isn’t food or attractions — it’s where you sleep, and specifically, which borough you sleep in. A hotel in Williamsburg or Long Island City runs 30–50% cheaper than the same quality room in Midtown, and you’re looking at a 15–20 minute subway ride into Manhattan. I’ve stayed both ways, and honestly, unless you’re planning to walk back to your hotel at 1 AM every night, Brooklyn is the move if you’re watching your budget.

Airbnb Warning

Under NYC’s Local Law 18, renting an entire apartment or house for fewer than 30 days is effectively illegal. If you’re looking at whole-apartment listings in Manhattan or Brooklyn, check the fine print carefully.

Getting Around the City

Quick and important update if you haven’t been in a while: the MetroCard is gone. Fully retired as of January 2026. Everything runs on OMNY now — you tap your phone, a contactless card, or a physical OMNY card at the turnstile. No more fumbling with a swipe machine that eats your card on the third try.

The fare is a flat $3 no matter how far you’re going. And here’s the part I actually love about the new system: it caps itself. Once you’ve paid $35 in a week using the same card or phone, every ride for the rest of that week is free. You don’t have to buy an unlimited pass upfront and hope you ride enough to make it worth it — it just automatically becomes free once you’ve hit the cap. Use the same payment method every single time or you’ll reset your progress.

Eating Without Losing Your Mind (Or Your Budget)

Food is where a trip quietly gets expensive, because no single meal feels like a splurge — it’s the accumulation that gets you. A $15 breakfast doesn’t feel like anything. Five of them in a row is $75 you didn’t plan for. My rule: pick one or two meals a trip that are actually worth the money, and keep everything else intentionally cheap and fast.

Tipping, Tax, and the Hidden 20%

Nobody budgets for this and everybody gets surprised by it. NYC sales tax is 8.875%, and it hits almost everything — restaurant meals, hotel rooms, most retail. On top of that, restaurants expect 18–20% tip on the pre-tax total. Some card readers now default to suggesting 25–30% — you don’t have to tap that button. Check the actual bill before you tip.

Tipping Guide
  • Restaurants: 18–20% of the pre-tax bill
  • Taxis and rideshare: 15–20%
  • Counter service: not required, but $1–2 is appreciated
  • Groups of 6+: many restaurants auto-add 18–20% — check before tipping again
Tax Reality Check

Budget an extra 8.875% on every purchase, plus tip on top of that at restaurants. On a $50 dinner per person, the real out-of-pocket is closer to $62 before drinks. Plan for 25–30% above menu prices at sit-down restaurants.

Where I’d Cut Corners — And Where I Wouldn’t

Not every dollar in this budget matters equally. Some things are genuinely fine to cheap out on. Others will make your trip worse if you do.

Cut here, no regrets

  • Hotel breakfast — $25 for eggs you can get for $6 at a diner
  • Souvenir shops right next to major attractions — walk two blocks, prices drop fast
  • Taxis for anything the subway covers in under 30 minutes
  • Bottled water from a cart — grab it at a bodega or drugstore for a third of the price

Don’t cut here

  • Your one Broadway show — see it once, do it right, don’t settle for a bad seat to save $30
  • A real dinner at least once — three average $20 dinners are worth less than one $60 dinner you actually remember
  • A hotel in a safe, well-connected location — saving $40/night isn’t worth an hour-long commute back at midnight

Money-Saving Moves That Actually Work

  • 1
    Stay in Brooklyn or Queens instead of Manhattan — a 20-minute subway ride can save you $100–200 a night on the same quality room.
  • 2
    Use the OMNY weekly cap on purpose — tap the same card every single ride and you’ll automatically stop paying once you hit $35 for the week.
  • 3
    Do the CityPASS math before buying — it only pays off if you’re hitting 3–4+ attractions. Run the numbers against individual ticket prices first.
  • 4
    Try the Broadway lottery for every show you’re interested in — even the ones you think you won’t win. It takes under a minute to enter and the odds are better than people assume.
  • 5
    Eat one real meal a day, keep the rest fast and cheap — you’ll spend less overall and actually remember the meal that mattered.
  • 6
    Buy an eSIM before you land instead of using an airport SIM kiosk or paying roaming charges. Airalo is the one I’d use — it’s a fraction of the cost and takes two minutes to set up.

NYC Trip Cost: Frequently Asked Questions

Is $150 a day enough for New York City?▾
Yes, for most people. $150–180 a day covers a budget hotel, mostly-subway transportation, one sit-down meal, and a paid attraction here and there. You won’t be doing Broadway and a tasting menu on this budget, but you won’t feel like you’re missing the city either.
How much should I budget for a week in NYC?▾
For a comfortable but not extravagant week, plan on $1,200–1,400 per person at the budget-conscious level, or $1,900–2,250 if you want a mid-range trip with a bit more breathing room. These figures exclude flights.
Is NYC more expensive than other major US cities?▾
Yes — New York consistently ranks as one of the most expensive US cities for hotels specifically, running noticeably higher than Chicago, Miami, or Washington DC for a comparable room. Food and attractions are also priced at a premium compared to most of the country.
How much cash should I bring to NYC?▾
Honestly, very little. Almost everything runs on card or phone tap now, including the subway (OMNY). Bring $40–60 in small bills for the rare cash-only spot or a tip in a pinch, and rely on card for everything else.
Can I do NYC on a budget without missing the highlights?▾
Yes, easier than people think. Central Park, the High Line, Brooklyn Bridge, and the Staten Island Ferry are all free and are genuinely some of the best things to do in the city. Mix those in with one or two paid attractions and you’ll hit the highlights without the $300/day price tag.
Do I need to tip in New York?▾
At sit-down restaurants, yes — 18–20% is the real standard, not optional the way it might feel elsewhere. At counter service and coffee shops, it’s appreciated but not expected. Don’t let a card reader pre-selecting 25% pressure you — check the actual total before tapping.

The Bottom Line

There’s no single right number for a NYC trip — there’s the number that matches the trip you actually want. If this is your first time and you just want to see the city without obsessing over every dollar, budget-conscious ($150–180/day) is where I’d point you. If you’re coming back for a special occasion, this is a city that rewards splurging on the right things — just not on all of them.

Whatever budget you land on, the biggest lever you have isn’t cutting corners everywhere — it’s picking one or two things to actually spend on and being ruthless about the rest.
